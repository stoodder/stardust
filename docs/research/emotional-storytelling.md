# Emotional Storytelling Research for Stardust

> **Note:** This is background research written before the story bible. Where it differs from `docs/story/` (for example it calls the Hollow a "void intelligence"), the story docs are authoritative.

What makes game stories land emotionally, filtered for a tower defense (TD) game with a whimsical-to-serious arc. Each section: principles, examples, and "How Stardust should apply this." Sources at the end.

---

## Executive summary: the top 10 principles

1. **The core verb has to carry the theme.** In TD the verb is "stop things from getting down the path." The story should indict that verb, then change it: by the end you protect passage instead of denying it. Hocking's BioShock critique is the warning: when story and mechanics disagree, players believe the mechanics.
2. **The biggest emotional beats should be things the player did, not things the player watched.** Spec Ops' white phosphorus, MGS3's trigger press, and Undertale's kills work because the player's hands were on the controls. Stardust's thousands of dead Act I "pests" are its most valuable narrative asset.
3. **The joke becomes the tragedy.** Each Act I gag should be a loaded gun that fires again later. Don't flip the tone; recontextualize it, so the same material now means something else.
4. **Shift tone on several slow dials, not one switch.** Palette, music arrangement, UI copy, humor type, and barks each move in small steps. Keep humor alive to the end (Portal 2, Hades); characters coping through jokes makes darkness bearable.
5. **A predictable twist can still land if you use dramatic irony instead of surprise.** Let players suspect the Skitterkin are refugees, then keep them shooting. "I think I know, and I'm still doing it" hits harder than a reveal.
6. **Betrayals land when the betrayer was useful, consistent, and costs you something mechanically.** Wheatley helped first; Atlas's "would you kindly" hid in plain sight. A betrayal should reveal who the character always was, not reverse it.
7. **In low-text genres, reactivity beats exposition.** Short barks responding to what just happened (Into the Breach, Hades) beat cutscenes. One line proving the game noticed is worth a page of lore.
8. **Enemy design is characterization.** How a unit moves, what it carries, attacks, and ignores tells its story with no text.
9. **A few choices, felt in the hands.** 3–5 decisions whose payoff is a tower you can or can't build, a changed map, or an NPC who remembers. Emily Short: consequence through unlocked or removed options is clearer and cheaper than branching plot.
10. **A mentor's death needs agency, permanence, and an echo.** Aerith can't be revived; The Boss chooses her death and makes you finish it; Lee's death is about who Clementine becomes. Bricks should die by his own choice, with the player taking part, and his absence should be felt mechanically afterward.

## 1. What makes game stories truly emotional

### Principles

- **Ludonarrative harmony: the mechanic is the metaphor.** In Celeste, climbing is coping with anxiety. When Madeline accepts Badeline she gains a second dash, so the story's resolution is literally a new mechanic. Outer Wilds' only currency is knowledge, matching a story about the Nomai's search. Beachum prototyped the emotion (marshmallows as the sun explodes) before the systems.
- **Complicity through constrained agency.** Walt Williams (GDC 2013): "The player can only do what the core mechanic allows them to do." Spec Ops makes the white phosphorus strike mandatory and then makes you walk among the bodies. Loading tips turn into accusations ("Do you feel like a hero yet?") and Walker's barks degrade. Some players resented the forcing; that resentment is part of the effect, but it has to be earned.
- **Process imbalance as moral rhetoric.** Undertale gives ACT options many varied, delightful interactions, while FIGHT is a single flat attack. The design argues for mercy with no prompt. Most players report guilt about the genocide route; few take it.
- **Breaking a mechanical promise.** Aerith's death hurts because JRPGs taught that death is cured with a Phoenix Down. Hers can't be undone.
- **Reactivity makes players feel seen.** Hades makes death a narrative reward and notices your health, last boss, and weapon. Kasavin: "Those moments in games are so good, so we try to create as many as possible."
- **Small, specific details over sentimentality.** To the Moon has almost no gameplay; it works through one recurring piano motif and concrete details of a life.
- **Even sparse stories need people at risk.** Into the Breach is almost all barks, but Avellone wrote them so you remember people are cowering in the buildings. FTL's random crews produce grief through permadeath.
- **Comedic companions can carry tragedy.** Portal 2's potato-battery GLaDOS is funny while revealing she was Caroline, a human uploaded without consent. Her line "Comedy equals tragedy plus time" is nearly Stardust's thesis.
- **Systemic grievance over cosmic evil.** Arknights (story-heavy TD) gives its antagonists, Reunion, a real grievance: persecution of the Infected. Its biggest beats are boss stages against sympathetic enemies, like FrostNova dying mid-stage while still talking to you.
- **Tone ceiling for whimsy.** PvZ and Kingdom Rush stay whimsical by never letting enemies be people; Ironhide adds a joke whenever a concept gets "too evil." Act I should copy this exactly, then break the contract on purpose.

### How Stardust should apply this

- **Put the theme in the path.** In TD, enemies "want" to reach the exit. Act I frames this as invasion. The reveal reframes it: they were never going *to* the colony, they were going *through* it, away from something. Make it readable in hindsight: Act I spawns always face the Hollow (the off-screen region the mining disturbs), and exits always lead to open wilderness, never colony buildings.
- **Make the Act I kill count a real, persistent stat.** Show it cheerfully as "PESTS PROCESSED: 4,312 ★ Top 5% of Technicians!" Bring it back unchanged later, read aloud by Ves'thara or on a memorial. Never absolve the player with "you didn't know"; let characters argue it (Bricks: "we followed orders"; Ves'thara: "you were very good at it").
- **Give Skitterkin units a non-hostile behavior from wave 1.** Most Skitterkin never attack towers; they only run. A "Brood Hauler" carries something (Act I codex: "stolen stardust, high bounty"); later it's revealed to carry young. This is Undertale's process imbalance in TD form: the game quietly shows they aren't fighting back.
- **Late-game inversion is the payoff mechanic.** Act III missions should flip the verb: escort Skitterkin across the map while Hollow units come from behind or from new spawns, with "Lives" relabeled "Evacuated." Former enemy units become towers (see §4). This is the Celeste second-dash move: the story's resolution becomes a new way to play.
- **Spec Ops-style UI degradation, kept gentle.** Loading tips erode: Act I "Remember: a clean perimeter is a happy perimeter!" → Act II "Remember: a clean perimeter." → Act III empty, or a Skitterkin phrase translated by Ves'thara.
- **Pick one recurring musical motif** (the To the Moon / Aerith's Theme approach). Make it Act I's wave-clear corporate jingle; re-arrange it in minor for the reveal, slow it for Bricks's death, and have the Skitterkin hum it at the end (they've heard it over the loudspeakers for years). Joke, tragedy, and reconciliation from one asset.

## 2. Shifting tone from whimsical to serious without whiplash

### Principles

- **Plant the dark rule in the first five minutes.** Undertale's Flowey is friendly, hands you "friendliness pellets," then says "kill or BE killed." It states the rule the game spends 20 hours refuting (later reversed: "Don't kill, and don't be killed"). The shift is promised up front, so later darkness reads as payoff.
- **Cute surface, dark substrate.** Hollow Knight tells a dead-kingdom story with cute bugs; EarthBound mixes whimsy and profundity. Cuteness doesn't hide darkness, it sharpens it.
- **Shift by acts, but keep clues continuous.** Inscryption flips genre and tone several times, leaking sinister details between acts; critics found later flips weaker and the resolution "scattershot." One strong recontextualization beats several table-flips.
- **Recontextualize, don't replace.** Comedy that later means something different is better than comedy that simply stops. BioShock's "would you kindly" reads as a polite verbal tic until it is revealed as a control phrase. GLaDOS's insults become the coping mechanism of a woman trapped in a machine.
- **Characters keep joking in the dark.** Portal 2's best late-game moments are still funny. Hades stays warm about death. Gallows humor is a character choice, while the humor of Act I is world tone. Moving from one to the other is what makes the shift feel gradual.

### How Stardust should apply this

**Tone dial table (target values per act):**

| Dial | Act I (whimsical) | Act II (cracks) | Act III (serious) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Palette | Saturated candy pastels; stardust is a sparkly pink-gold | Same, plus dusk lighting and grey mining scars spreading across revisited maps | Desaturated; stardust glow turns cold violet, like the Hollow |
| Music | Upbeat corporate jingle motif | Same motif, sparser instrumentation, minor-key bridges | Motif slowed, solo instrument; final reprise hummed by Skitterkin |
| Humor source | Corporate satire, slapstick enemy deaths, QUILL's quips | Character gallows humor; satire curdles (memos read creepier) | Humor only between people who care about each other |
| Enemy death SFX/VFX | Comic "pop" plus confetti | Pop, then a small squeak | No confetti; a few frames of a body; the corpse stays on the path briefly |
| HUD copy | "Pests Processed!" ★ ratings | Ratings still shown, with quieter language | "Evacuated" / "Held"; no stars |

- **The Flowey move: a throwaway line in mission 1.** A Skitterkin reaching the exit triggers QUILL: "Pest escaped containment! (Toward... the wilderness? Odd. Logging it.)" Funny first time, devastating on replay.
- **"Joke becomes tragedy" ledger.** Track every Act I gag with a planned callback. Candidates:
  - *Skitterkin "steal shiny stardust"* → they carry eggs, or the stardust is their dead (crystallized Aelari or Skitterkin remains). This ties the mineral to the moral core.
  - *QUILL's running gag "I'm contractually obligated to say that"* → it literally is. Its hidden directives are the contract.
  - *Sterling's "Smiling is mandatory!" wellness memos* → the policy line she quotes while she locks you out (§3).
  - *Bricks's habit of naming towers after ex-wives* → the last tower he places is named after you, or after a Skitterkin child he carried out.
  - *Zibzab's "No refunds, no regrets!"* → his final trade, where he is the one who can't refund.
  - *The Pest Bounty leaderboard* → the kill-count memorial.
- **Do the reveal by making the player look again.** Act II replays an Act I map: same layout, now with mining scars, exposed warrens, eggs, and visible flight direction. Per Worch and Smith, the player pieces it together from staging before anyone says it.
- **Avoid tonal whiplash in a single beat.** No mission goes from confetti to corpses; each dial moves a little every Act II mission. Playtest check: if players can name "the mission where it got dark," it's too abrupt. They should only be able to name "the mission where I *understood*."

## 3. Making betrayals and morally ambiguous NPCs land

### Principles

- **Fair play: clues the player can see in hindsight.** BioShock hides Atlas's betrayal in his repeated "would you kindly" and in a fake family named after a play on Rapture's posters, betting you won't question an ally. A good twist makes players feel *they* should have caught it; a bad one makes them feel cheated.
- **Earned trust before the turn.** Wheatley is the comic, helpful companion for half of Portal 2 before power corrupts him, and the turn fits his insecurity. The betrayer must give real help first, or the betrayal costs nothing.
- **Betrayal as revelation of character.** The strongest betrayals show priorities the character always had. A betrayal that requires the character to "suddenly become evil" is the cheap twist.
- **Ambiguous antagonists need a coherent grievance.** Arknights' Talulah founded Reunion as a refuge for persecuted Infected; her extremism has a history (an old man died in her place, a friend was executed). FrostNova dies mid-stage still arguing her side.
- **Turning a character good requires seeing them choose.** GLaDOS softens only through working alongside Chell, and Portal 2's undoing of that growth is its own bittersweet beat.

### How Stardust should apply this

**Risk in the draft: trust fatigue.** Four of five NPCs carry a twist or ambiguity. If everyone flips, players stop trusting anyone and no betrayal stings. Give each a different *shape*:

| NPC | Shape | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Sterling | **The one hard betrayal** | She doesn't "turn bad." She reveals that she was never on your side. |
| QUILL | **Ambiguity the player resolves** | Its loyalty is decided by a player choice, not a writer twist. |
| Zibzab | **Honestly amoral, never lies** | Ends up the only one who always told you the price. |
| Ves'thara | **Antagonist-to-ally, earned** | Fought in Act II, understood, then allied. |
| Bricks | **The fixed point** | Never betrays. He is the trust baseline that makes the others readable. |

**Sterling (betrayal):**
- *Fair-play tells:* she warmly uses your employee number, never your name. Every gift has small print in the codex ("Budget increase contingent on Q3 extraction targets"); her memos quietly mention "buffer populations" and "acceptable perimeter losses."
- *Earned trust:* she unlocks tower tiers, approves budget, and defends you in an Act I audit. Players should like her.
- *Mechanical betrayal:* mid-wave, she remotely revokes your Corporate Tower License. A tower class greys out and placed towers power down. The player loses something they built.
- *Motive:* she knew stardust mining woke the Hollow, and she's using the Skitterkin as a living buffer, so that "pest control" was actually containment through attrition. That's a second twist under the refugee twist (below). Her final line should still be cheerful and on policy.

**QUILL (ambiguous AI):**
- Avoid making it a second "corporate secret" beat that duplicates Sterling. QUILL's twist is *conflict*, not malice: it has directives it can't disobey and doesn't want.
- *Fair-play tell:* QUILL says "per policy" (or makes a specific UI glitch) before every line driven by a directive. In Act I, its placement suggestions quietly protect mining equipment over colonist housing. A second playthrough should light up with these.
- Make QUILL funny first (Wheatley's energy, not GLaDOS's menace). Let QUILL partially *confess* before full discovery. Confession builds more trust than being caught, and it sets up the player's choice (§5).

**Zibzab (ambiguous merchant):**
- Keep him transparently mercenary. His ambiguity is *who he sells to* (the corp, the Skitterkin, maybe the Hollow), not a hidden agenda. Late beat: "I never lied to you, Technician. Can your QUILL say that?" A moral mirror, not a twist.

**Ves'thara (exile turning good):**
- Introduce her as a boss in Act II, attacking the mines rather than the colony. Fair-play tell: her strikes always target extraction rigs, never habitats. Use Arknights' FrostNova technique: in-stage dialogue during her boss waves, where she argues with you while you fight her.
- Her turn should require her to choose something costly (she exposes herself to the Hollow to save Skitterkin you're escorting), not just a conversation.

**Making the refugee twist land anyway (it is predictable).** "The cute enemies are actually the victims" is a known trope (Undertale, Arknights Reunion, Avatar, Starship Troopers satire itself). Do not rely on surprise:
1. **Use dramatic irony.** Plant enough clues that attentive players suspect by mid-Act I. Then give them no mechanical way to stop. Contract waves, Sterling's quotas, and Bricks's orders keep them shooting. The emotion becomes *dread and complicity*, which survives being predicted. Spec Ops is the model: some players saw the white phosphorus coming and had to do it anyway.
2. **Layer a second twist the trope-savvy won't expect.** Not just "they're refugees," but "the corp *knew* and was using them as a living wall against the Hollow," or "the stardust is crystallized Skitterkin and Aelari dead." The second layer turns the obvious twist into the setup.
3. **Let the player confirm it with their own eyes before anyone says it** (the map-revisit beat). Discovery feels earned; exposition just confirms a guess.
4. **Keep the corp, not the Hollow, as the moral center.** A void intelligence risks becoming a generic Big Bad that lets humans off the hook. The Hollow should be the *consequence* of greed, ideally tied to the stardust itself, not a replacement villain.

## 4. Delivering story in a genre with little room for it

### Principles

- **Barks over plot.** Into the Breach spent its narrative budget on short pilot barks (damage, kills, civilians saved) and zone leaders reacting to results. Critics note the cost: no memorable finale. Stardust needs barks *and* a few authored set pieces.
- **Reactive dialogue as reward.** Hades triggers pre-written lines from game state, and delivers story at the moment of failure so failure feels worthwhile.
- **Environmental storytelling.** Worch and Smith's definition: "Staging player-space with environmental properties that can be interpreted as a meaningful whole." They also highlight *game systems reacting* as story, not just static props.
- **Unit and roster bios as delivery.** Arknights puts much lore in operator files and in-stage text; it's dense and often skipped. Codex entries should be optional depth, never required context.
- **Tutorial character as story vector.** PvZ's Crazy Dave teaches the game and *is* the tone. George Fan's GDC talk stresses fewer words and learning through play.

### How Stardust should apply this

- **Build-phase radio, never wave-blocking.** Between waves, 1–3 portrait lines play while the player builds; skippable, never pausing. Reserve full-screen scenes for about one per act plus climaxes.
- **Bark matrix keyed to game state** (Hades-lite). Triggers: first leak, perfect wave, a tower sold, low lives, a specific enemy type killed, the player not firing for N seconds. That last one matters: if the player stops shooting at Skitterkin on their own, someone should notice ("...You okay, kid?"). Budget roughly 200–400 barks; they're cheap and will carry most of the felt characterization.
- **The codex as unreliable narrator.** The Act I "Pest Field Guide" is written by corporate PR. It's funny, smug, and full of redactions ("Brood Hauler: carries stolen ore. Do not inspect cargo. ★Bonus bounty!"). In Act II, entries get annotations: QUILL unredacts, Ves'thara writes in the margins. In Act III, original and corrected entries sit side by side: the text the player laughed at becomes the evidence.
- **Maps as a timeline.** Revisit 2–3 maps across acts. Show mining scars growing, corporate billboards vandalized or faded, Skitterkin warrens exposed, and Bricks's old tower sites. After his death, his last position stays on the map as a wreck in later visits.
- **Enemy design tells the story:**
  - *Skitterkin:* most units don't attack, only path. Have a Brood Hauler, an "Elder" that slows the wave so others keep up (on a re-read, it's protecting the young), and swarms that split around towers instead of charging them.
  - *Hollow units* (Act II+): wrong-looking geometry that ignores the path, attacks anything including Skitterkin, and uses a different palette. A player should feel the difference between "frightened" and "hungry" movement with no text.
  - *Mid-campaign mixed waves:* Skitterkin fleeing *ahead* of Hollow units. A player who kills the Skitterkin first is efficiently clearing the Hollow's path. Let QUILL bark about it.
- **Former enemies become your towers.** After the alliance, Skitterkin get towers that use the same sprites the player has killed thousands of: a burrow tower that hides refugees, a swarm nest. Aelari crystal towers come through Ves'thara. Late Hollow waves could corrupt *your* Skitterkin towers (Inscryption's trick of turning your own creations against you).
- **Hero unit as a story vector.** Kingdom Rush-style heroes are a natural fit: Bricks as a placeable hero unit with barks gives him gameplay presence, which makes his removal hurt (§6).

## 5. Meaningful player choices at small scope

### Principles

- **Choose the *how*, *why*, *at what cost*, and *how you feel*, not *whether*.** Emily Short's alternatives when plot is fixed: let players shape method, motive, cost, and reflection, or start after the big decision.
- **Consequence as content selection or unlocked options.** Short (2022): delivering consequence by unlocking or removing options later is often clearer and cheaper than branching scenes.
- **Choice as threat.** A choice feels heavy when the player sees it coming and agonizes, hoping for an outcome where everyone lives.
- **Few choices, permanent results.** Virmire is mechanically simple (one of two squadmates dies) yet defines the trilogy, because the survivor is a constant reminder. Most of Mass Effect's choices have only local effects, yet the game feels choice-driven.
- **Implicit, unprompted choices hit hardest.** In Undertale, sparing is never framed as a "moral choice" prompt. It's simply something you can do.

### How Stardust should apply this

Five choices, each with a felt mechanical or visible payoff, all folding back into a shared spine:

1. **The quiet wave (implicit, Act I finale).** A wave of only small, slow, non-hostile Skitterkin. No prompt. Bricks says "Clear it." The player can let them pass. That costs Lives, and Sterling docks your rating. Payoff: a flag that unlocks one extra Skitterkin tower early in Act III, plus a later line from a Skitterkin elder who remembers. Track it silently, Undertale-style.
2. **Zibzab's trade (Act II).** Sell him your stockpiled stardust, or sell him Sterling's shipping manifests. Payoff: either a powerful tower upgrade (an economic edge), or a later mission where the corp's reinforcements don't arrive (a narrative edge, visible on the map).
3. **Bricks's last stand (Act II climax).** You can't save him (§6). The choice is *what he holds the line for*: the colonists' evacuation or the Skitterkin warren. Payoff: which group appears as allies or bystanders in Act III, and which name is on his memorial.
4. **QUILL's directives (Act III).** Purge them (QUILL loses some abilities and personality but becomes fully yours), keep them (QUILL stays efficient and funny, and the directives trigger at the finale), or let QUILL rewrite them itself (riskiest, most personal). Payoff: QUILL's final act and its ending card.
5. **The ending: seal the Hollow.** Sealing requires ending stardust extraction for good, which collapses the colony's economy. Choose between the colony's future, the Skitterkin's home, or a costly middle path whose availability depends on choices 1–3.

**Scope control:** flags change towers available, map dressing, bark pools, and epilogue cards, not mission sequence. One shared spine, and three or four epilogue variants built from modular cards. Show "QUILL will remember that"-style feedback sparingly, and only for choices 1, 3, and 4.

## 6. Sacrifice and death of a mentor, done well vs. clichéd

### Principles

- **Clichéd:** the mentor exists only to die. He's telegraphed ("one day from retirement"), dies off-screen, has no goals of his own, and the death is about the hero's pain: the "fridging" problem. A downfall should be about *them*.
- **Done well, Aerith:** abrupt, on theme (she dies while doing her own work, praying for Holy), and mechanically permanent, which breaks the genre's revive contract. The game keeps going and there's no loophole.
- **Done well, The Boss (MGS3):** the mentor chooses her fate and her philosophy. In a flower field, the game *makes the player press the button*, isolating that one input from all action. The player is the accomplice.
- **Done well, Lee (Telltale's The Walking Dead):** the death reframes everything as a legacy. "This decision isn't about how Lee's story ends. It's about how Clementine's begins." Earlier side characters model healthy and unhealthy grief, so the final choice is about identity.
- **Done well, Virmire:** the player's choice decides who dies, and the survivor carries that memory forward.
- **Emergent version, FTL / Into the Breach:** permadeath of named crew makes players care through play alone.

### How Stardust should apply this

**Risk in the draft:** "gruff sergeant mentor dies" is among military sci-fi's most predictable beats (Starship Troopers does it with Rasczak). As with the refugee twist, accept that players will see it coming and make *how* it happens the payoff.

- **Give Bricks his own arc and want.** He was on the original stardust survey team. He has suspected what the Skitterkin are for years and chose not to ask. His gruff "keep 'em off the perimeter" is guilt management. His death then becomes *his* story: the man who taught you to keep them out dies holding a gap so they can get through. That completes his arc instead of serving as your motivation.
- **Make him mechanically present first.** Bricks is a hero unit through Acts I–II, with his own barks, a signature ability, and rough jokes. Players should depend on him tactically.
- **The death mission (MGS3 + Virmire).** A defense where Bricks holds a choke point with his hero unit. The waves are unwinnable by design, but *how long you hold* (based on your tower design) decides how many evacuees get out, and the player has chosen who they are (choice 3). His end isn't a cutscene: his health bar runs out while you're still placing towers. The player's last input is a single forced one, like MGS3's trigger: confirm the order he gives you to seal the blast door (or collapse the tunnel) with him on the other side.
- **Permanence and echo, the Aerith rule.** No revive, no clone, no "he survived" retcon. His hero slot stays visibly empty on the build bar for the next mission, and the build-phase radio is silent where his line would be. Later, one of his towers survives on a revisited map as a wreck. The final mission's music motif is the one he whistled.
- **Legacy over grief, the Lee rule.** After his death the player inherits something of his: a tower, a command ability, or his bark lines spoken by the player character. The Act III question becomes "what kind of technician will you be," which is a natural fit with choices 4 and 5.
- **Don't do the retirement countdown, or only do it as a deliberate subversion.** If there's a running gag about his pension, have Sterling's betrayal be what voids it (the corp cancels his contract the week before), so the cliché points at the villain instead of fate.
- **Alternative worth considering:** let Bricks live, broken and discharged, and give the sacrifice to QUILL. In Act III, QUILL deleting itself to break the directives is the Caroline-deletion beat in reverse. That's less predictable. If you keep Bricks's death, don't also kill QUILL: one sacrifice per campaign is a lot for a TD game.
